# KidneyShield: Project Journey, Python Research Pipeline, and NIH ReGen Challenge Alignment

## The Story Behind KidneyShield

KidneyShield began with a deceptively simple question:

**Could an existing, affordable medicine help slow chronic kidney disease—and could a reproducible evidence workflow help identify which treatment hypotheses deserve closer scientific investigation?**

Chronic kidney disease places a substantial burden on patients, families, healthcare systems, and public resources. Although effective treatments exist, researchers continue to investigate additional strategies for protecting kidney function and reducing the risk of disease progression.

Drug repurposing offers one possible avenue. Medicines already in use have existing pharmacological and safety information, and published research may contain evidence relevant to conditions beyond their original indications. However, that evidence is scattered across scientific publications, clinical-trial registries, supplementary materials, and other sources.

Finding a promising study is relatively easy compared with establishing what it actually demonstrates.

A paper may mention a medicine and a kidney outcome without demonstrating a treatment benefit. An automated extraction may capture a number without its comparator, statistical uncertainty, or study population. A promising result may also be contradicted by another study.

KidneyShield was developed around this evidence gap.

Rather than treating drug repurposing as a search for the medicine with the most mentions, the project builds a structured workflow for finding candidate records, organizing research signals, prioritizing records for human review, and preserving the connection between extracted information and original sources.

The ambition is to make early-stage drug-repurposing research more transparent, inspectable, and reproducible.

## From an Idea to a Research Workflow

The development journey can be understood through several stages.

### Stage 1 — Define the scientific question

The project focuses on chronic kidney disease, a condition explicitly identified as a priority area in the NIH ReGen Drugs Evidence Package Challenge.

The research question is not simply whether a medicine has ever been associated with kidney function. It is whether the available evidence supports a credible hypothesis for a new or expanded therapeutic use.

### Stage 2 — Identify a candidate research direction

Pentoxifylline became an important candidate for investigation because researchers have studied its possible effects on kidney-function decline and urinary albumin excretion in diabetic kidney disease.

The PREDIAN randomized trial provides a concrete starting point for examining clinical evidence.

However, one trial is not a complete evidence package. The study must be interpreted in context, compared with other evidence, and assessed for safety, applicability, and remaining uncertainties.

### Stage 3 — Build a structured evidence workflow

The project developed a sequence of screening and extraction stages that produce inspectable intermediate outputs rather than a single opaque result.

These outputs include source indexes, evidence matrices, drug–outcome co-mentions, study-design summaries, numerical candidate extractions, and manual-verification queues.

This structure allows researchers to investigate how a record entered the workflow and what should be checked before its findings are used in a scientific argument.

### Stage 4 — Turn data into interpretable research artifacts

KidneyShield includes CSV tables and visualizations covering the evidence-screening process.

The project also includes kidney anatomy and pathology images to explain the biological context of CKD. These educational images are deliberately distinct from the figures generated by the research workflow.

### Stage 5 — Make uncertainty visible

A key development principle is to separate candidate signals from verified findings.

The repository includes an explicitly unverified evidence matrix and dedicated verification queues. These artifacts help make the remaining work visible rather than presenting automated extraction as completed clinical validation.

### Stage 6 — Move toward a defensible evidence package

The next phase is to strengthen the manually verified evidence base, examine the complete benefit–risk picture, assess the consistency of clinical findings, and document gaps against the official NIH ReGen requirements.

The project journey is therefore not a claim that a drug has been proven effective. It is a progression from a research question toward a more rigorous and reviewable assessment of the evidence.

## Technology Stack

### Python

Python is the principal programming environment for the project's computational research workflow.

It is well suited to collecting research records, processing structured information, organizing candidate evidence, producing analytical summaries, and generating scientific visualizations.

The public notebooks and scripts should be treated as the authoritative record of which libraries and methods were actually used in each stage.

### Jupyter and Kaggle notebooks

Notebook-based development supports a step-by-step research workflow in which individual stages can produce inspectable outputs.

This approach is useful for exploratory research because it allows intermediate tables, extraction results, and figures to be reviewed without hiding the entire process inside a single application.

The exact notebook execution environment, dependencies, and data-access requirements should be documented alongside each reproducible release.

### Tabular research data

CSV files are used to make selected intermediate outputs accessible outside the notebook environment.

This supports inspection using Python, spreadsheet software, or other data-analysis tools.

The separation between source records, evidence matrices, review queues, and summaries also makes it easier to identify where a result originated and what additional checks it requires.

### Scientific visualization

The project contains figures for evidence volume, study-design signals, outcome screening, trial status, review priorities, and research workflow status.

Visualization makes patterns easier to inspect, but every chart must be interpreted in light of its underlying data and calculation method.

### Research-source traceability

Source identifiers, publication links, and trial-related cross-references are important parts of the workflow.

The goal is to allow reviewers to move from an extracted research signal to the underlying publication or registry entry whenever the source information is available.

### Reproducible research philosophy

KidneyShield prioritizes a workflow in which intermediate outputs are inspectable and uncertain records remain identifiable.

A published notebook is not automatically reproducible. Exact inputs, retrieval dates, software versions, extraction rules, and execution instructions must also be preserved for independent reproduction.

## How the Computational Workflow Operates

The project's research process can be summarized in seven steps.

1. **Collect:** identify candidate scientific publications and clinical-trial records.
2. **Screen:** examine records for relevance to kidney disease, medicines, and outcomes of interest.
3. **Extract:** organize relevant terms, study characteristics, and candidate numerical findings.
4. **Classify:** summarize study-design and outcome signals where the available information permits.
5. **Prioritize:** arrange selected records into review queues.
6. **Verify:** compare extracted claims with the original sources.
7. **Interpret:** assess the findings, limitations, conflicting evidence, and remaining research needs.

The distinction between automated screening and human verification is fundamental.

For example, a record mentioning pentoxifylline and eGFR may warrant review. Before that record can support a conclusion, a researcher must establish whether the study actually evaluated pentoxifylline, which population was studied, what comparison was made, how eGFR changed, and whether the results support the proposed interpretation.

KidneyShield is designed to help organize this process—not replace scientific judgment.

## Quantitative Analysis and Scientific Figures

Quantitative analysis can make an evidence package stronger when it is applied to appropriately extracted and verified data.

KidneyShield's existing visual assets provide a starting point for describing the screening workflow and its intermediate outputs.

The current figure collection includes:

* Retrieval-volume summaries.
* Kidney-related endpoint maps.
* Study-design signal distributions.
* Trial-status summaries.
* Hard-outcome screening views.
* Review-priority distributions.
* Publication-year distributions.
* Candidate-drug landscape summaries.
* Evidence-workflow status figures.
* Microscopy-image quality-control and contact-sheet visuals.

These figures serve different analytical purposes.

**Workflow statistics** describe the operation of the research pipeline.

**Evidence-composition statistics** describe the types of records collected or classified.

**Clinical effect estimates** describe observed differences in patient outcomes and require appropriate study-level data, statistical interpretation, and uncertainty reporting.

These categories must not be conflated. A large evidence set does not necessarily mean that a treatment has strong evidence, and the number of mentions of a medicine does not estimate its treatment effect.

Where sufficient comparable clinical data are available, future analyses should examine effect estimates, confidence intervals, heterogeneity, study quality, and the consistency of findings across independent studies.

## Connection to the NIH ReGen Drugs Evidence Package Challenge

**Official challenge:** [Repurposing Generic (ReGen) Drugs Evidence Package Challenge](https://www.nih.gov/challenges/repurposing-generic-regen-drugs-evidence-package-challenge)

KidneyShield is being developed in a research area that aligns with the purpose of this challenge: investigating whether existing generic medicines may have additional uses for conditions with a substantial healthcare burden.

The NIH National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences (NCATS) leads the ReGen Drugs program, with support from the NIH Chief Artificial Intelligence Officer within the NIH Office of the Director.

The challenge seeks evidence packages that identify promising generic drug-repurposing opportunities. The broader objective is to help generate evidence that could support future clinical testing, updates to FDA-approved labeling, or inclusion of new uses in relevant compendia when the evidence justifies those steps.

### Why the challenge matters

Many generic medicines have established uses, but there may be limited commercial incentive to fund studies of additional indications once patent protection and market exclusivity have expired.

This can leave potentially useful research questions insufficiently developed, even when preliminary evidence exists.

The ReGen program aims to help identify and prioritize opportunities where additional evidence could support more affordable treatment options for costly conditions.

CKD is one of the challenge's explicitly listed priority conditions.

The challenge page describes a competitive evidence-package process, not simply a competition to build an AI model or create an attractive dashboard.

### What the challenge expects

The official submission requirements call for an evidence package that addresses the proposed new use of a medicine, including:

1. Scientific rationale and mechanism of action.
2. Benefit–risk considerations.
3. Relevant non-clinical evidence.
4. Safety, pharmacology, and pertinent adverse effects.
5. Clinical and/or real-world evidence.
6. Pharmacogenomic considerations where relevant.
7. Proposed treatment regimen and drug–drug interactions.
8. Additional research needed before clinical or regulatory adoption.
9. References that allow the reader to locate the underlying evidence.

The challenge specifies a one-page overview and a 14-page evidence package, with references excluded from the page limit. Participants should follow the complete official requirements when preparing a submission.

The challenge also requires a clinical signal of efficacy beyond an individual case report or a single institution's experience. A computational screening result alone is not enough.

### Why KidneyShield is relevant

KidneyShield's evidence organization and source-verification workflow can support the early stages of preparing such a package.

| Challenge need                         | KidneyShield contribution                             | Remaining work                                                                         |
| -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Identify a candidate drug–disease pair | Screens and organizes candidate records.              | Establish that the proposed indication meets challenge requirements.                   |
| Find relevant clinical evidence        | Collects literature and trial-related records.        | Verify completeness and identify missing or conflicting studies.                       |
| Evaluate clinical outcomes             | Organizes kidney-related outcome signals.             | Extract and assess study-level results, effect estimates, and limitations.             |
| Examine study quality                  | Includes study-design summaries and review queues.    | Complete a defensible assessment of bias and evidence strength.                        |
| Assess safety                          | Provides a framework for organizing research records. | Assemble authoritative prescribing information, safety evidence, and interaction data. |
| Support reproducibility                | Publishes tables and research artifacts.              | Document complete methods, dependencies, inputs, and validation.                       |
| Prepare an evidence package            | Provides a structured evidence-discovery foundation.  | Produce the full clinical, pharmacological, safety, and benefit–risk assessment.       |

**Alignment is not the same as eligibility or competitiveness.** KidneyShield's existing workflow is a starting point for evidence preparation, not proof that a completed submission already meets the challenge's standards.

### The pentoxifylline question

The PREDIAN trial provides relevant clinical evidence for investigating pentoxifylline in diabetic kidney disease.

However, the official challenge places importance on the strength of the clinical evidence, the proposed new indication, the risk–benefit balance, and the status of the medicine as a generic drug without unexpired patents or exclusivity.

Before selecting pentoxifylline for a formal challenge submission, the project needs to verify:

* Whether the proposed drug–indication pair satisfies the challenge's requirements.
* The current patent and exclusivity status relevant to the proposed use.
* How commonly the drug is already prescribed off-label for the proposed indication.
* The complete body of clinical and real-world evidence, including negative or contradictory findings.
* The safety profile, dosing considerations, and drug interactions in the intended population.
* Whether the evidence establishes a sufficient clinical signal and a credible benefit–risk assessment.

The challenge identifies certain commonly prescribed off-label uses as lower-priority opportunities. Therefore, the existence of PREDIAN alone does not establish that pentoxifylline is the most competitive candidate.

The responsible next step is to assess the candidate against the complete official criteria before making a formal selection.

## What Success Would Look Like

For KidneyShield, success should mean more than identifying a large number of candidate records.

A stronger research deliverable would enable an independent reviewer to:

* Understand why a drug–disease pair was selected.
* Locate the original studies supporting the hypothesis.
* Reproduce the reported numerical findings where the data permit.
* Compare clinical evidence across different study designs.
* Understand the known and uncertain risks.
* Identify contradictions and gaps in the evidence.
* Determine which additional studies would materially change the conclusion.

The ultimate aim is to make a treatment hypothesis easier to scrutinize and, when justified, easier to advance toward appropriate clinical investigation.

## Development Roadmap

### Phase 1 — Research infrastructure

**Current foundation:** structured source records, evidence matrices, screening queues, review-priority outputs, and research figures.

The priority is to stabilize the workflow and ensure that outputs can be traced to the relevant sources.

### Phase 2 — Primary-study verification

Review high-priority records against their original publications and trial registrations. Standardize extraction of study design, patient population, treatment regimen, comparator, outcomes, follow-up, effect estimates, uncertainty, and limitations.

### Phase 3 — Clinical evidence synthesis

Evaluate the consistency and quality of the evidence. Distinguish surrogate endpoints from hard clinical outcomes and document the effects of study design, sample size, missing data, and potential bias.

### Phase 4 — Benefit–risk assessment

Assemble relevant pharmacology, safety, contraindications, warnings, interactions, and dosing information. Determine which gaps require additional clinical or non-clinical research.

### Phase 5 — Challenge-readiness review

Compare the completed evidence package with every official challenge requirement. Verify candidate eligibility, source citations, independent evidence, and all submission materials.

### Phase 6 — Reproducible public release

Publish the final verified data tables, methods, analysis code, figures, dependencies, and known limitations so that independent researchers can inspect the work.

These phases are planned research priorities, not a claim that each stage has already been completed.

## Responsible Research and Medical Disclaimer

KidneyShield is an independent research project. It is not an NIH-funded or NIH-endorsed project, a medical device, a validated clinical decision-support system, or a substitute for professional medical advice.

The presence of a medicine in the research workflow does not imply that the medicine is recommended for CKD. No patient should start, stop, or change medication based on the repository.

The project's purpose is to support transparent evidence discovery and evaluation. Clinical conclusions must be based on verified evidence and an appropriate assessment of effectiveness, safety, and uncertainty.
